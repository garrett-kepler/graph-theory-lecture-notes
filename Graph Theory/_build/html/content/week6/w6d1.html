Lecture 12: Bipartite Graphs#

Warmup#

Distance#

Definition 36 (Distance)

The distance between two vertices \(u\) and \(v\) in a graph \(G\) is the length of the shortest \(uv\)-path in \(G\). We typically denote this \(\text{dist}_G(u,v)\) or simply \(\text{dist}(u,v)\) when the graph we are talking about is clear.

Some conventions regarding distance:

  1. \(\text{dist}(u,u)=\begin{cases} &0~~\text{if there is no self-loop}\\ &1~~\text{if there is a self-loop} \end{cases}\)

  2. \(\text{dist}(u,v)=\infty\) for vertices with no path between them

Theorem 19

Let \(T\) be a tree. There are at least two vertices of degree 1 in \(T\).

Proof. Let \(T=(V,E)\) be a tree. Let \(u\) and \(v\) be two vertices such that \(\text{dist}(u,v)\) is the largest in \(T\). Recall there is only one path between vertices in trees. So, assume that one of these vertices, say \(u\), has degree larger than one (i.e. \(d_T(u)>1\)).

Then, there exists an edge \(\{u,w\}\) \underline{not} on the path from \(u\) to \(v\). This makes the \(vw\)-path formed by the \(uv\)-path and the edge \(\{u,w\}\) \underline{longer} than \(\text{dist}(u,v)\). This is impossible since \(\text{dist}(u, w)\) cannot be larger than \(\text{dist}(u,v)\) by assumption. So, this extra edge \(\{u,w\}\) does not exist and \(d_T(u)\leq 1\). If \(d_T(u)<1\), then the tree would be disconnect. So, \(d_T(u)=1\).

Since this same argument applies to \(v\), we know at least \(u\) and \(v\) will have degree 1.

Bipartite#

Definition 37 (Bipartite Graphs)

A graph \(G=(V,E)\) is bipartite if each vertex in \(V\) can be placed into two disjoint, \(U\) and \(W\), such that no vertex in \(U\) shares an edge with a vertex in \(U\) and likewise with \(W\). We sometimes call \(U\) and \(W\) partitioning sets or a bipartition.

Another way to think of bipartite graphs: two disjoint independent sets with (potentially) edges between them.

Theorem 20

A graph \(G\) is bipartite if and only if \(\chi(G)\leq 2\).

Proof. Let \(G=(V,E)\) be a graph.

\rightarrow Assume \(G\) is bipartite with partitioning sets \(U,W\subseteq V\).

If \(G\) has no edges, \(\chi(G)=1\) since there are no edges anyway!

Otherwise, color \(U\) blue and \(W\) red. Since \(U\) and \(W\) are independent sets by definition, no edge has two vertices of the same color. That is, \(\chi(G)=2\). So, \(\chi(G)\leq 2\) in general.

\leftarrow Assume \(\chi(G)\leq 2\).

If \(\chi(G)=1\), then every vertex has no edges with any other vertex (i.e. \(G\) is the empty graph!). The empty graph is bipartite!

If \(\chi(G)=2\), then there is some 2-coloring of \(G\) such that \(U,W\subseteq V\) are independent sets colored different colors. So, these independent sets \(U\) and \(W\) are also a bipartioning.

Thus, \(G\) is bipartite if and only if \(\chi(G)\leq 2\).

Theorem 21

Trees are bipartite.

Proof. Let \(T=(V,E)\) be a tree. Take \(v_0\in V\). We have shown there is a unique path from \(v_0\) to every other vertex.

Let \(U\) be the vertices \(u\in V\) such that \(\text{dist}(v_0,u)\) is even. Let \(W\) be the vertices \(w\in V\) such that \(\text{dist}(v_0,w)\) is odd.

Assume \(v_1\) and \(v_2\) are both in \(U\) or both in \(W\) and the edge \(\{v_1,v_2\}\) exists. Again, there is a unique \(v_0v_1\)-path since \(T\) is a tree. Note that taking this \(v_0v_1\)-path and then the edge \(\{v_1,v_2\}\) forms a \(v_0v_2\)-path. Again, this \(v_0v_2\)-path must be unique. So, we know \(\text{dist}(v_0,v_2)=\text{dist}(v_0,v_1)+1\). So, these distances can’t be both even or both odd. So, \(\{v_1,v_2\}\) doesn’t exist or these vertices are not in the same set.

Thus, \(T\) is bipartite.
